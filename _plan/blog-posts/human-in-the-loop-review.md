I've been to Ranchi once. It seemed like many other Indian towns I'd been to. Travelling from town to town in India in the oughts they all seemed to blend into each other, the same bus stops and the dusty bridge or canal would seem like a minor variation in scenery that added a little charachter but by and large they all blended into small variations on a common theme.

Later when I started travelling internationally I had a similar feeling about airports and airplanes throughout the world, they were all about the same. Nothing much changed from city to city, transfer to transfer. The world was indeed flat. Flatter than I thought it would be and certainly flatter than I wished it to be.

Not unlike Chesterton's yachtsmen who leaves England with great fanfare to look for adventure only to accidentally rediscover his homeland I too found my way back the domestic and personal adventure, not in my homeland but in the great trials of my heart.

---

Watching the short and powerful movie "Humans in the Loop", set in the forests beyond the boundary of  small town in rural Jharkhand an hour outside Ranchi is a providential raking over the coals for my heart. The story is about bringing texture to the flat world of AI. Like with all art it is wrapped up in the artists ideology in this case a local variant of eco-feminism. Today I'll look past that veneer and focus on the heart of the movie as it reflected somethings I've wrestled with in my own heart.

The founding trauma of the plot is the break down of a legally unrecognized common-law marriage, a custody dispute and a broken family's struggle through a very modern situation with very modern tensions set against the backdrop of lush forested hills littered with caves full of tribal art. 

The protagonist Nehma an Oraon tribal has left her husband with a pre-teen daughter and an toddler and moved back to the outskirts of her childhood village after 10 years living with him in Ranchi. Her husband is a forward caste man whose family has rejected her. Her dignity is offended the romantic fires have cooled into seething embers of resentment and she seems to have drugged her daughter during her escape. Throughout the movie her anger toward her husband is unquenchable, and enflamed by his decision to marry another woman since their marriage isn't legally registered he doesn't need to legally dissolve it.

A hundred years earlier, this simple sequence played itself out in my family. My great grand mother, Magdalena a tribal Christian woman from the Himalayan foothills around Darjeeling also married a forward caste Mukherjee man in the late British Empire. She moved with him to Rangoon then the capital of British Burma and had a child Regina, my grandmother. Sometime in the 1930s she was widowed and driven out of her marital family with her daughter to start a new life in the urban jungle, Calcutta. The psychological fires lit these lit continue to rage through the lives of her descendants into the 21st century.
